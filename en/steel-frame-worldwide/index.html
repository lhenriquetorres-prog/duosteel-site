History and data

Steel frame worldwide

Light steel frame — cold-formed galvanized steel profiles — was born in the United States almost a century ago. Today it frames hotels, hospitals, schools and homes across North America, Japan, Australia and Europe. Here is its story, in numbers and with sources.

What light steel frame is

It is a structural system made of galvanized steel profiles 0.8 to 2.3 mm thick, cold-formed into shape. [8] Studs, tracks and joists form wall panels, floors and roofs, which are sheathed with cement board, gypsum or OSB and filled with thermal and acoustic insulation.

In the United States it is called cold-formed steel framing (CFS). It is dry construction: no curing, no rubble, and parts arrive from the factory cut and numbered. That makes construction faster and more predictable.

Timeline

A century of light steel

  1. c. 1925

    Virginia Baptist Hospital, Lynchburg, Virginia: the first documented use of cold-formed steel in a building, with joists made of back-to-back C sections. A recent renovation found they still carry their loads. [1]

  2. 1933

    At the Chicago World’s Fair, “A Century of Progress”, three steel-framed houses — General Houses, Armco-Ferro and Stran-Steel — are shown as the home of the future. [1]

  3. 1939

    The American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI) sponsors research at Cornell University to understand how thin-gauge steel behaves. [1]

  4. 1946

    AISI publishes the first specification for the design of light-gauge steel structural members. From then on, the system is designed to a standard. [1]

  5. 1948–1950

    Lustron mass-produces 2,498 steel houses. It goes bankrupt in 1950 with more than 8,000 orders unfilled: the demand was there, the industrial scale was not. About 1,500 still stand. [5]

  6. 1950s

    Self-drilling screws replace “nailable” studs and screw guns arrive: erection becomes fast and precise. [1]

  7. 1960s

    As buildings grow taller, light steel moves into curtain walls, brick-veneer facades and shaft walls: it is light and non-combustible. [1]

  8. 1994

    After years of high, volatile lumber prices, a study for the US Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) compares real projects: steel walls cost only about 7% more than wood, mostly due to less experienced crews. [4]

  9. 1995

    Great Hanshin earthquake (Kobe, Japan): of about 50,000 temporary homes, some 3,000 are built with imported steel framing. In 1996, six Japanese steelmakers set up a committee to develop the system. [6]

  10. 2004

    81% of interior walls built in the US already use steel studs. [1]

  11. 2005

    Brazil publishes the ABNT NBR 15253 standard for light steel framing profiles. The system had arrived in the country in the early 1990s. [8]

  12. 2011

    By 2011, structural stud tonnage in the US surpasses non-structural: light steel is no longer just partitions — it holds buildings up. [1]

  13. 2016

    Duosteel starts designing, fabricating and erecting steel structures in Paraguay and Brazil, combining structural steel and light steel frame.

  14. 2025

    About 5,000 single-family homes completed in the US are steel-framed: 25% more than in 2024. [2]

United States

Where the system became an industry

In the US, cold-formed steel is above all a commercial construction system: 30 to 35% of nonresidential buildings use it for structure or enclosure. [1] It is common in hotels, mid-rise apartment buildings, schools, hospitals and retail.

The market matured with its own standards and industrial fabrication. Today, high-strength studs (made with higher-yield steel and stiffening ribs) account for 90% of the studs used in the country, carrying the same loads with thinner steel. [1]

In single-family housing, by contrast, wood dominates: in 2025, 94% of completed homes were wood-framed and 0.5% steel-framed, about 5,000, up 25% from the year before. [2] In Paraguay and Brazil the comparison is different: here steel framing competes with masonry and concrete, not with wood, and that is where its short schedule and dry construction pay off.

Worldwide

How other countries use it

Japan

After the Kobe earthquake, steelmakers developed the system and brought it under the Building Standard Law in the early 2000s. By 2005, 2,157 buildings had been built, with about 20,000 units. A three-story steel-framed apartment building weighs about 100 kg/m²; a reinforced concrete one, about 1 t/m². [6]

Australia

Has used light steel framing in homes since the 1940s. It is especially popular in areas with high termite risk, although the house still needs termite protection. [7]

Europe and New Zealand

Together with the US, Japan and Australia, these are the markets where the system is most widespread, according to Brazil’s Inmetro. [8]

Brazil

Light steel frame arrived in the early 1990s and has had its own standard since 2005. In 2010 it held about 1% of the construction market: still a young system next to masonry. [8]

Paraguay

Duosteel offers light steel frame in Paraguay, with in-house engineering and erection crews. In Brazil it has already used the system for houses and commercial spaces, such as the light steel frame house in Foz do Iguaçu.
